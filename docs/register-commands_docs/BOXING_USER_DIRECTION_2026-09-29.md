**1. How to classify a boxing style**

The original material mixes several different layers. Keeping them separate makes the information more useful for training and film analysis.

| Layer | What it describes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategic archetype** | How a fighter generally creates advantages and wins exchanges. | Out-boxer, boxer-puncher, pressure fighter, slugger, counterpuncher. |
| **Guard or defensive structure** | How the hands, arms, shoulders and torso protect targets. | High guard, cross-arm guard, Philly shell, long guard. |
| **Footwork method** | How a fighter enters, exits, changes angles and controls distance. | Pendulum stepping, pivots, lateral steps, stance shifts. |
| **Stance** | Which side leads, and whether the fighter changes that relationship. | Orthodox, southpaw, switch-hitting. |
| **Integrated system or training tradition** | How several mechanics are taught together. | Cus D’Amato’s peek-a-boo approach; particular Soviet or Cuban coaching traditions. |
| **Individual style** | A fighter’s particular combination of mechanics, tactics, physical attributes and habits. | Jones’s explosive entries, Augustus’s broken rhythm, Durán’s infighting. |

A fighter can belong to several categories simultaneously. **James Toney**, for example, combines counterpunching, pocket fighting and shoulder-roll defence. **Mike Tyson** combines pressure, explosive counterpunching and peek-a-boo mechanics.

“Unique” here means **distinctive and recognisable**, not that a fighter invented every technique they used.

**2. Core strategic archetypes**

| Archetype | Core strategy | Typical mechanics | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Out-boxer / pure boxer** | Control distance and score while limiting the opponent’s opportunities to exchange. | Jabs, straight punches, lateral movement, pivots, step-backs and controlled entries. | Does not require constant circling or fighting exclusively on the back foot. An out-boxer can advance while preserving preferred range. |
| **Boxer-puncher** | Combine technical control with meaningful finishing power. | Structured combinations, accurate placement, timing, jabs that establish power shots, and transitions between outside and mid-range. | A broad hybrid category, not automatically the “best” or most complete style. |
| **Pressure fighter / swarmer / infighter** | Restrict the opponent’s space, close distance and sustain productive exchanges. | Ring cutting, guarded or evasive entries, body work, short punches and combinations. | These terms overlap but are not identical: pressure describes spatial control; swarming emphasizes activity; infighting describes close-range skill. |
| **Slugger / brawler** | Make exchanges decisive through damaging punches and physical pressure. | Committed power shots, hooks, overhands, uppercuts and sometimes a heavy straight right or jab. | Slow hands, poor footwork and accepting punishment are not requirements. Power punching can be technically sophisticated. |
| **Counterpuncher** | Exploit an opponent’s attack, recovery or predictable reaction. | Feints, traps, catches, parries, slips, pulls, interception and immediate returns. | Does not simply wait. A counterpuncher can lead, pressure and provoke the punch they intend to counter. |

**3. Defensive structures, footwork methods and integrated systems**

**Peek-a-boo**

Closely associated with **Cus D’Amato**, with **Mike Tyson** and the historical example **Floyd Patterson** among its best-known practitioners.

- **Guard:** Hands held high around the cheeks, with forearms protecting the front and elbows managing body exposure.
- **Movement:** Coordinated knee and hip action moves the head off the centreline through slips, ducks and weaves.
- **Footwork:** Short entries, lateral shifts and angle changes help close distance while creating punching positions.
- **Offence:** Defensive movement connects directly to hooks, uppercuts and body-to-head combinations.
- **Purpose:** Reduce the usefulness of an opponent’s reach while entering a range where compact punches are effective.

The system is more than a high guard. Its defining feature is the integration of guard, head movement, footwork and counterattack. “Non-stop bobbing” is an exaggeration: movement needs timing and purpose, and does not automatically remove a taller fighter’s reach advantage.

**Cross-arm defence**

Associated with **Archie Moore**, **Ken Norton** and **George Foreman’s comeback period**. **Joe Frazier** also used related crossed-arm positions, although his crouch, weaving and pressure are more defining features of his overall style.

- **Structure:** Forearms cross or overlap in front of the body and head, with their height and orientation changing according to the threat.
- **Defence:** Punches can be caught, redirected or absorbed on the arms, supported by torso movement and positioning.
- **Offence:** The fighter must uncross or reposition an arm to punch, making the transition between defence and attack important.
- **Purpose:** Provide a compact protective structure while remaining close enough to counter or advance.

It does not automatically block every hook or body shot. Openings depend on arm position, the opponent’s angle and the transition between guard positions. Fast overhands are one possible problem, not its single defining weakness. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

**Philly shell and shoulder roll**

Strongly associated with **Floyd Mayweather Jr.** and **James Toney**.

- **Guard:** In a common orthodox configuration, the lead arm lies across the torso, the lead shoulder protects the chin, and the rear hand remains available to catch or parry.
- **Shoulder roll:** Torso rotation changes how an incoming punch meets the shoulder and upper body.
- **Supporting defence:** Rear-hand catches, small slips, pulls, changes of level and footwork cover threats the shoulder cannot handle.
- **Counters:** Rear-hand counters, short uppercuts and lead hooks can follow the defensive action.

The **shell is a guard structure**; the **shoulder roll is an action**. They are related, but not interchangeable terms.

Against another orthodox fighter, rolling a right cross over the lead shoulder is a classic application. Jabs can be handled through catches, parries, slips and other responses. The entire defence cannot accurately be reduced to “the shoulder deflects jabs.”

Mayweather also used high guards, long-range movement and other defensive configurations. No position keeps all targets permanently hidden. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

**High guard**

**Winky Wright** is a particularly clear example.

- **Structure:** Hands protect the head while elbows and forearms adjust to cover the body.
- **Defence:** Compact blocks and catches reduce the need for large evasive movements.
- **Offence:** Jabs and straight punches launch from a relatively protected position.
- **Purpose:** Maintain defensive organisation while moving forward, holding position or returning fire.

A high guard requires active adjustment. It can still be split, displaced or attacked around and underneath.

**Pendulum footwork**

**Dmitry Bivol** is a strong study example; **Oleksandr Usyk** also uses rhythmic changes of distance within a broader movement system.

- **Movement:** Controlled movement into and out of range, coordinated with the stance and punching action.
- **Attack:** Enter during a favourable beat, deliver punches and leave before the opponent completes their response.
- **Defence:** Change distance enough to make a punch fall short while retaining the ability to return.
- **Rhythm:** Establish a pattern, then vary its timing, amplitude or direction.

The goal is functional distance control, not bouncing continuously or remaining “perfectly balanced” under every circumstance. Bivol’s pendulum action is one component of his jab, combinations and positioning. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

**Soviet and Cuban boxing traditions**

The attachment’s label **“Soviet/Cuban Pendulum”** combines distinct things too tightly.

Both traditions have produced fighters with sophisticated footwork, distance judgment and straight punching, but neither is a single uniform style. Pendulum stepping is a technique; Soviet and Cuban boxing refer to broader coaching traditions with considerable variation.

**Bivol** is useful for studying rhythmic entries and exits. **Guillermo Rigondeaux** is useful for studying southpaw positioning, economical movement and counter timing. They should not receive identical technical labels simply because both control range well.

**Hands-low, movement-led boxing**

Examples include **Roy Jones Jr., Naseem Hamed, Emanuel Augustus, Sergio Martínez**, and the historical reference **Muhammad Ali**.

- **Defence:** Distance judgment, anticipation, head movement and feet compensate for reduced static hand coverage.
- **Offence:** Punches can begin from unusual positions or arrive after unexpected changes of rhythm.
- **Deception:** Invitations, feints and apparent openings encourage predictable attacks.
- **Movement:** Weight transfers and torso positioning connect defence to attack.

These fighters do **not** share one standardized “hyper-reflexive system.” Their mechanics and tactical priorities differ. Nor did they permanently abandon conventional guards.

**Long guard and lead-hand control**

Useful examples include **George Foreman, Lennox Lewis, Tyson Fury** and aspects of **Usyk’s** lead-hand work.

- The lead hand can measure distance, obstruct vision, touch or redirect an opponent’s glove, and establish the jab.
- The rear hand and body position support the extended lead arm.
- Hand contact can help the fighter recognise or influence an opponent’s movement.

An extended “mummy” or long guard is **not the same structure as a cross-arm guard**. A fighter may transition between them. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

**8. Using the note for training and film analysis**

For each exchange, record the observable action before assigning a style label.

| Observation field | What to record |
|---|---|
| **Fighter and career phase** | The particular version of the fighter being studied. |
| **Bout context** | Opponent, round, timestamp, relative reach and stance matchup. |
| **Starting range** | Outside punching distance, long range, mid-range or close range. |
| **Stance and guard** | Actual position at the start of the exchange, including changes. |
| **Initiating action** | Jab, feint, step, touch, pressure, invitation or opponent attack. |
| **Defensive response** | Catch, parry, slip, roll, block, pull, pivot or retreat. |
| **Footwork** | Entry, exit, lateral step, pivot, shift or stance switch. |
| **Offensive sequence** | Punch order and target changes. |
| **Position afterward** | Balanced to continue, safely outside, trapped, squared up or requiring recovery. |
| **Outcome** | Clean connection, blocked shot, miss, counter received or reset. |
| **Interpretation** | The apparent purpose of the sequence, distinguished from what is directly visible. |

For example, **“counterpuncher”** is a broad label. **“Draws the jab with a forward feint, pulls outside its range, returns a straight right, then exits laterally”** describes a sequence that can actually be studied and compared.

When reviewing the original stylistic groups:

- **Unconventional movement and rhythm:** Jones, Hamed, Augustus, Whitaker and Martínez.
- **Guard-based defence and pocket counters:** Mayweather, Toney and Wright.
- **Mobile combination boxing and angle changes:** Pacquiao, Lomachenko, Usyk and Calzaghe.
- **Pressure, ring cutting and close-range craft:** Tyson, Durán, González, Golovkin and Hopkins.
- **Long-range heavyweight control and power:** Lewis, Fury, Vitali Klitschko, Wilder and Foreman.
- **Stance, timing and economical positioning:** Crawford, Rigondeaux, Márquez and Bivol.

These groups are an index for study. They deliberately overlap.
